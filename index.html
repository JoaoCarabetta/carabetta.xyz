public data should be infrastructure, not paperwork.

the idea

Brazil produces extraordinary public data — censuses, elections, budgets, schools, hospitals, every formal company and every formal job. but for decades it lived scattered across hundreds of portals, each with its own formats, encodings, broken links and undocumented quirks. the data was technically public and practically inaccessible. access was rationed by suffering: only people with time and engineering skills could use it.

the idea behind Base dos Dados is that access to quality public data should behave like infrastructure — like a road: built once, maintained centrally, free at the point of use. we clean the datasets once, standardize the identifiers so tables actually join with each other (a municipality is the same municipality in the census, the election and the budget), document everything, and put it all in one warehouse anyone can query with plain SQL.

the trick that made it economically possible: hosting the data lake on BigQuery, where the consumption cost is paid by the user — and since every user gets a free monthly quota, most people pay nothing. a small nonprofit could suddenly serve an entire country without owning a single server. marginal cost of a query: approximately zero.

where it started

in 2015, during a strike at UNICAMP, i co-founded UNICAMP Transparency — a student collective that used data to answer what the strike was really about and how deep the budget cuts went. coordinating fifteen contributors and defending analyses in front of assemblies of five hundred people taught me the founding lesson: transparency is not a document, it's a capability.

what exists today

Base dos Dados became a national reference for open data in Brazil — hundreds of treated datasets, client packages in python, R and stata, and millions of queries served. the methodology is written up in Data Basis: Universalizing Access to High-Quality Data (dahis, carabetta, scovino, israel & oliveira, 2022).

a city that can see itself can act before problems become crises.

the idea

governments are usually blind in real time. the data exists — buses broadcast GPS, schools take attendance, clinics log visits, citizens open tickets — but it arrives late, trapped in silos, in formats nobody can join. so the city governs by anecdote and quarterly report. my work in Rio was about closing that loop: turning the city's own exhaust of records into a live, governed, queryable picture of itself.

where it started: the buses

in 2021 i joined Rio's transport department to lead data science. the first request sounded small: Rio's buses had carried GPS since 2011, but the feed was barely used. we built the pipeline to capture and process it — every ping, every line, every operator — so the city could finally answer basic questions: which bus passes where, at what headway, and whether the companies were delivering the service they were paid for. one live feed, honestly captured, changed the terms of an entire public service conversation.

data office → the data lake

that pipeline became the seed of the Data Office, the city's data office, which i founded and ran. in june 2022 we launched Rio's municipal data lake: a single governed platform centralizing the operational data of the whole city government — transport, health, education, citizen services. it grew past 4.7 billion records, feeding real-time dashboards managers actually use: classroom attendance, health visits, tickets on the 1746 citizen hotline. google cloud later featured the office among its global public-sector cases, alongside governments of the uk, netherlands and finland, the us air force and nasa.

running the machine: iplanrio

from there i became the city's chief data officer, then chief executive of technology, and in 2025 president of iplanrio — Rio's municipal technology company, founded in 1979, responsible for the systems the city runs on: the 1746 hotline, carioca digital, processo.rio, and the health network's information systems, including opening the city's health statistics through tabnet. the job of a public technology company, as i see it: make the invisible plumbing so reliable that citizens never have to think about it. i led iplanrio until 2026.

the principle

real-time data changes the tense of government. a city that only sees the past can only apologize; a city that sees the present can dispatch a repair crew, call a family whose kid stopped showing up to school, fix the bus line this week. that shift — from reactive to proactive — is worth more than any dashboard.

cities speak geometry. tools should make geometry cheap.

the idea

almost every hard question about a territory — where to put a clinic, which streets flood, who lives more than thirty minutes from a school — is secretly a geometry problem. and geometry, in practice, is where analyses go to die: incompatible grid systems, gigantic polygons that crash your queries, boundary files scattered across government FTP servers. i build small, sharp open-source tools that remove exactly those frictions, so that the analyst's afternoon is spent on the question, not the plumbing. what you're seeing behind this text is the core trick: the same territory, readable at every resolution.

the tools

babelgrid — the world has several competing ways of dicing the planet into cells (google's S2, uber's H3, quadtrees…). babelgrid is one python API that speaks all of them, so you can pick a grid system the way you pick a unit of measure. built and published open source with the Inter-American Development Bank.

geobr — official Brazilian spatial datasets (municipalities, states, census tracts, biomes…) one function call away, in R and python. i maintain the python side of this IPEA project — one of the most-used geospatial libraries in the country.

osmpy — a wrapper around openstreetmap's overpass API that lets you query regions of any size and shape; it includes a "katana" routine that slices huge polygons into digestible pieces so big-area queries stop timing out.

isometric — walking-distance polygons from any point in a city: the honest answer to "what is actually nearby?"

data pipelines with the same spirit

at the Inter-American Development Bank i helped build the coronavirus impact dashboard: during the pandemic, we turned waze's traffic feeds into a daily congestion index for cities across Latin America — a near-real-time measure of how much life had stopped, published openly while the crisis was still unfolding. the same season, as a volunteer, i worked on farolcovid, an open tool monitoring the risk of hospital collapse in Brazilian municipalities.

the principle

open source is public policy by other means. a good library is a subsidy paid to every future analyst, in every city, forever. i would rather ship a boring, reliable tool that a thousand strangers quietly depend on than a flashy report nobody can reproduce.

reduce a messy system to the few forces that matter.

the idea

i trained as a physicist at UNICAMP, and physics left me one permanent habit: in any complicated system — a galaxy, a bus network, a procurement office — most of the variables are noise, and a handful of forces explain almost everything. the craft is finding those forces, modelling them honestly, and not being intimidated by the mess around them. instead of a physics phd, i took that habit to FGV/EMAp, where i did a master's in mathematical modelling — at the time the only program in Brazil teaching modern applied math with its hands on real policy problems.

congress, in numbers

during the master's i worked at FGV's Center for Technology and Society, publishing data analyses in the national press — including Congress in Numbers, a project that treated the national congress itself as a dataset: votes, speeches, amendments, money. the lesson: institutions become legible when you count them, and legibility changes how they behave.

procurement, in probabilities

in 2019 i was a fellow at the Alan Turing Institute's data science for social good program (with the University of Warwick), working with Paraguay's national procurement agency, the DNCP. the agency reviews roughly 13,000 public tenders a year with about thirty officials and a three-day window per tender — attention is the scarcest resource in the room. we built a model that ranks incoming tenders by risk of irregularity, so reviewers spend their limited hours where the taxpayer's money is actually in danger. anti-corruption work, done as a prioritization problem.

development, in evidence

as a consultant at the Inter-American Development Bank's office of strategic planning and development effectiveness, i worked on turning novel data sources into evidence for policy across Latin America — most visibly the waze-based coronavirus impact dashboard, and analyses of pandemic-era air quality in the region's cities.
